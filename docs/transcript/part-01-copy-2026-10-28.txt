The passage gets things mostly backwards, though there’s a real point buried in it.

“Virtual photons” isn’t a placeholder for ignorance. It’s a precise term from quantum electrodynamics (QED), the quantum theory of electromagnetism. In QED, the thing that carries the electromagnetic force is the electromagnetic field, and photons are its quantized excitations. When physicists calculate how two charged particles interact, they use Feynman diagrams, and the internal lines connecting the particles get called “virtual photons.” That’s a specific mathematical object with exact rules attached to it. QED is also arguably the most precisely tested theory in science: its prediction for the electron’s magnetic moment matches experiment to roughly one part in a trillion. You don’t get that from a theory that has “absolutely no idea” what’s going on.

“Appears to obey the same laws as light” isn’t a mysterious coincidence. Light is the electromagnetic field. Of course the carrier of the electromagnetic force behaves like light; they’re the same thing. Ironically, virtual photons are the one place the resemblance breaks down. Real photons always satisfy the relationship between energy and momentum for a massless particle (E = pc). Virtual photons don’t. Physicists call them “off-shell.” That’s part of what lets them produce attraction as well as repulsion, and lets the Coulomb force come out of the math.

It also doesn’t generalize to other forces. The weak force is carried by the W and Z bosons, which are heavy, so the force only reaches very short distances. The strong force is carried by gluons, which interact with each other and cause quark confinement. Neither behaves much like light. Gravity is the real open question: we have no confirmed quantum theory of it, and the “graviton” is still hypothetical.

What the passage gets right. There is genuine debate about how literally to take virtual particles. Many physicists see them as bookkeeping in a particular approximation method (perturbation theory), not tiny objects actually flying between particles. The popular image of two particles tossing a ball back and forth is a loose metaphor, and it can’t really explain attraction. Beneath that, there are open questions about what a quantum field fundamentally is, but those are questions of interpretation and philosophy, not gaps in our ability to describe and predict the forces.

So a fairer version would be something like this: we have an extremely accurate theory of how electromagnetic forces work, phrased in terms of quantum fields. “Virtual photons” is a useful but easily misunderstood way of picturing part of the math, and what’s ultimately “real” underneath is still debated.

"we have absolutely no idea what it is that carries forces between two distant particles"

i was quite interested in this part

That line touches one of the oldest questions in physics, so it’s worth unpacking.

The problem is old. Newton’s theory of gravity had the Sun pulling on the Earth instantly across empty space, with nothing in between. Newton himself was uncomfortable with this. In a letter he called the idea of one body acting on another through a vacuum, with nothing mediating it, an absurdity no competent thinker would accept. He said he didn’t know the mechanism and wouldn’t guess. So for a long time, “we have no idea what carries the force” was an accurate description of gravity.

Then fields came along. In the 1800s, Faraday and then Maxwell developed the idea that space around a charge or magnet isn’t empty. It’s filled with a field, and the field is what pushes on other charges. At first this could look like a relabeling: “the field did it” instead of “it just happens.” But fields turned out to have real, measurable properties that settle the question:

They take time to travel. If you wiggle a charge, a distant charge doesn’t feel it instantly. The disturbance spreads outward at the speed of light. Something is physically crossing the gap.
They carry energy and momentum. Sunlight is a traveling electromagnetic field, and it heats things and physically pushes on them (radiation pressure is how solar sails work). Energy that has left the Sun and not yet arrived at Earth has to be somewhere, and the field is where it is.
They exist independently of their sources. Light from a star that burned out long ago is still traveling toward us. The field outlived the thing that made it.

So for electromagnetism, the answer to “what carries the force between distant particles?” is the electromagnetic field, and that answer is backed by measurements of its speed, energy and momentum. Quantum theory adds that the field’s energy comes in discrete lumps (photons), but the carrier is still the field.

Gravity got the same treatment later. Einstein’s general relativity replaced Newton’s instant pull with curvature of spacetime, which also propagates at a finite speed. Gravitational waves were directly detected in 2015, and in 2017 a neutron star collision showed gravitational waves and light arriving at almost exactly the same time after traveling 130 million light-years. Gravity’s influence travels too, at the speed of light.

What’s genuinely unanswered is a deeper question: what is a field? What is it “made of”? Physicists in the 1800s assumed waves needed a medium, and proposed the “luminiferous aether.” Experiments failed to find it, and relativity made it unnecessary. The modern view is that fields are fundamental. They aren’t made of anything more basic, as far as we know. Some people find that unsatisfying, and fair enough, but it’s a different claim from “we have no idea what carries the force.” It’s like the difference between not knowing who delivered your package and knowing exactly which courier did it, but not knowing what the courier is made of at the deepest level.

One more thing, since “distant particles” can call it to mind: quantum entanglement does involve correlations between far-apart particles that aren’t explained by anything traveling between them. But entanglement isn’t a force. It can’t be used to push, pull or send a signal, so it doesn’t reopen the action-at-a-distance problem for forces.
